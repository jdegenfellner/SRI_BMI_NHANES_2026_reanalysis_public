\subsection{Hand-over of the rebuilt series to the packages}
The published \texttt{sleepreg} pipeline targets \texttt{GGIR}~2.0-0 and is incompatible with the current \texttt{GGIR} (3.3-6). The \texttt{sleepreg} SRI engine was therefore run directly from its \texttt{GGIR}-independent source functions on the binary series. \texttt{sleepreg} reads the clock time of a recording from its first timestamp and fails when that timestamp falls exactly on midnight. Its copy of each timeline began at the first retained day with all timestamps offset by 30 seconds, which leaves its 10-second grid and its noon-to-noon days unchanged. The days before the first retained day are entirely missing in the shared timeline and their omission cannot change the result. \texttt{sleepreg} computes the SRI over the continuous recording at a fixed 24\,h lag, without matching by weekday, drops days with more than 6\,h missing and gives no value to participants with fewer than 5 valid comparison days at its default (\texttt{minSRIdays}).

\texttt{GGIR}'s \texttt{CalcSleepRegularityIndex} computes a Phillips SRI for each pair of consecutive calendar days, matched by time of day at 1-min epochs and using the valid epochs of each pair. It then averages the day-pair values into one SRI per person. The function assigns a value of zero (instead of a missing value) to a day pair without valid epochs. The day pairs were therefore averaged only where at least 66\% of the epoch pairs were valid, which approximates the package's own criterion (\texttt{frac\_valid} above 16/24). Averaging all day pairs without it would have given a median SRI of $43.8$ on the released NHANES series and a correlation of $0.76$ with the Phillips formula instead of $0.993$. Since version 3.2-6 (April 2025, as noted by Czeisler et al. [20]) \texttt{GGIR} distinguishes two outputs. SRI1 is computed from the epoch-level sleep classification, optionally after a rolling-window smoothing. SRI2 (part~6) is computed from the detected sleep period, with awakenings shorter than \texttt{SRI2\_WASOmin} (30\,min) counted as sleep. SRI2 requires \texttt{GGIR}'s own sleep-period detection from raw accelerometry, which NHANES does not release. Its input logic was emulated by the bridged input variant. The \texttt{GGIR} changelog records no change to the SRI between versions 3.3-6 and 3.3-9.

For the comparison with \texttt{pyActigraphy} [13] on the synthetic recordings and on 300 NHANES recordings, the missing minutes (non-wear or unknown) were filled with the preceding state, and at the start of a recording with the following state, for all four implementations. The 300 recordings were those with the fewest missing minutes (at most 2.8\% of minutes) among participants with seven consecutive retained days and a mean sleep share between 15\% and 60\%.

\section{Reproduction of the published SRI}
\label{sec:S_recon}
When the published filters were applied to the public minute files and the Phillips formula was recomputed, the resulting SRI correlated $0.96$ with the published values (Figure~\ref{fig:S_published}). The scores of 23\% of participants matched to within 0.01 points. For 59\% the difference exceeded one point. The rebuilt series is the output of the archived filtering code of the earlier study: running that code unchanged on the public files returned, for every participant, the same minutes, the same missing minutes and the same retained days as the rebuild. Applying the archived pairing rule to this output returned the rebuilt SRI exactly. The published values themselves could be reproduced exactly, for all 4879 and 5657 scored participants of the two survey cycles, with two earlier states of the same code that are kept in the original project. Both states pair minute $i$ with minute $i+1440$ in recording order and omit the Saturday-to-Sunday day pair, which the archived rule includes. The omitted pair affects 63\% of participants in 2011--2012 and 90\% in 2013--2014 and removes a median of 17\% and 15\% of their day pairs, respectively. The 2011--2012 state further required five days of data where the archived rule requires three. The 2013--2014 state had no 1440-minute rule, kept the partial days of placement and removal, and dropped participant-days with 120\,min or more of non-wear instead of dropping participants. With partial days in the sequence, 87\% of its participants have day pairs whose two minutes differ in clock time, for a median of 17\% of their pairs. For the 7076 participants with both scores, the published SRI exceeded the rebuilt one by 0.4 points on average and the medians were $61.5$ and $61.3$. Of these participants, 79\% fell into the same quintile. The code that reproduces the published values exactly is included in the companion repository.

\section{Synthetic recordings and recording gaps}
\label{sec:S_synthetic}
The packages were run on five deterministic synthetic recordings with known SRI (identical days, a sleep block shifted by 30 minutes on alternate days, a drift of 15 minutes per day, a nap on alternate days, night sleep alternating with day sleep), on a seeded random pattern and on a seven-day recording with realistic night-to-night shifts and naps. The deterministic recordings and the random pattern were presented complete and with one middle day retained as missing minutes. The realistic recording was presented complete, with the middle day retained as missing minutes and with that day deleted from the series. As in the main analysis, day pairs from \texttt{GGIR} were averaged only where the proportion of valid epoch pairs was at least 0.66.

On the complete recordings the Phillips formula, \texttt{GGIR} and \texttt{sleepreg} all returned the known SRI (Table~\ref{tab:S_synthetic}): $100$ for identical days, $91.67$ for the 30-minute alternating shift, $95.83$ for the 15-minute daily drift, $95.83$ for the nap on alternate days, $-33.33$ for night sleep alternating with day sleep, and less than one point from $0$ for the random pattern. The formula and \texttt{GGIR} agreed to three decimals. \texttt{sleepreg} differed by at most $0.4$ points because of its 10-second grid and its noon-to-noon days. \texttt{pyActigraphy} returned the same seven values to four decimals (\texttt{synthetic\_pyact.py}). On the realistic recording all three calculators returned the same value ($73.84$). On the 300 NHANES recordings with the fewest missing minutes, the formula, \texttt{GGIR} and \texttt{pyActigraphy} agreed to three decimals and \texttt{sleepreg} to $0.02$ points (median $71.9$ for all four).

When one middle day was missing, the result depended on how the gap was presented to the package. With the day retained as missing minutes, the formula and \texttt{GGIR} agreed ($68.75$), whereas \texttt{sleepreg} returned no value at its default minimum number of comparison days, because a missing calendar day removes two of its noon-to-noon days (with the minimum lowered to two days it returned $66.20$). With the day deleted from the series, the same recording gave $71.94$ from \texttt{GGIR}, which pairs each date with the next date present and so compared two days that were 48 hours apart. \texttt{sleepreg} gave $35.18$, because its transition format carries the last state before the gap over the deleted day. Neither package issued a warning. The published implementation of the formula pairs days by weekday index, skips the gap and returns $68.75$ in both presentations. A further detail of \texttt{GGIR} concerns the day pairs that its inclusion criterion rejects: these carry the function's initial value of zero instead of a missing value. An unfiltered average over all day pairs ($45.83$ for this recording) is therefore not equivalent to the result of the package's own pipeline.

\texttt{GGIR}'s own unit test \texttt{test\_CalcSleepRegularityIndex.R} builds six days at 5-second epochs with sleep blocks at varying positions and expects an SRI of 80 for day pairs 2 and 3, 100 for a constant series and $-100$ for a strictly increasing one. Through the wrapper used in this study the function returned 80.000 and 80.000, 100 and 100, and $-100$ and $-100$. The minute-level analogue returned 80 for every day pair, equal to the Phillips formula on each pair.

\section{Input variants, calculator agreement and the association with BMI}
\label{sec:S_inputs}
Figure~\ref{fig:S_inputdiag} shows the released classification at the epoch level, Figure~\ref{fig:S_sridist} the distribution of the SRI under each input variant, and Figure~\ref{fig:S_agreement} the agreement of \texttt{GGIR} and \texttt{sleepreg} with the Phillips formula on each input. Given the same timeline, \texttt{GGIR} and the Phillips formula differed by $0.08$ points on average (SD of the difference $2.0$; $r=0.993$). \texttt{sleepreg} differed from the formula by $0.02$ points at its default minimum of five comparison days (SD $0.3$; $r=0.9998$) and by $0.03$ points at three days (SD $1.7$; $r=0.995$). The agreement was the same among the 60\% of participants with seven consecutive recorded days and among the 40\% with fewer than seven consecutive days ($r=0.993$ and $0.992$ for \texttt{GGIR}). \texttt{sleepreg} returned no value for 3412 of the 7085 participants at its default and for 1743 at three days.

On the released series the Phillips formula gave a median SRI of $61.3$ (interquartile range $48.5$ to $71.1$). The rolling majority raised the median to $69.1$, \texttt{GGIR}'s SRI1 smoothing to $69.7$, the bridged series to $67.6$ and the main sleep period alone to $75.5$ ($64.7$ to $83.3$). The mean number of sleep--wake transitions fell from $17.7$ per day to between $2.1$ and $4.5$, whereas total sleep per day stayed between $7.2$ and $8.6$ hours, except under the 80\% rule, which removed approximately 100 minutes of sleep per day. The smoothed scores remained correlated with the score on the unsmoothed series ($r=0.97$ for the majority filter and the bridged series, $0.89$ for the 80\% rule, $0.75$ for the main sleep period alone).

Table~\ref{tab:S_assoc} gives the SRI--BMI multiplication factors for every input variant and calculator. In the fully adjusted model the factor was $0.955$ \CI{0.936}{0.976} on the released series, $0.964$ \CI{0.946}{0.983} after the majority filter, $0.969$ \CI{0.952}{0.986} after \texttt{GGIR}'s smoothing, $0.963$ \CI{0.945}{0.982} after bridging and $0.972$ \CI{0.955}{0.989} for the main sleep period alone. In women the corresponding factors were $0.922$, $0.933$, $0.936$, $0.932$ and $0.946$, in men $0.987$ to $1.002$. On every input the fully adjusted factor from \texttt{GGIR} differed from the factor of the formula by at most $0.002$ and the factor for women by at most $0.005$. The factors from \texttt{sleepreg} were $0.009$ to $0.020$ lower, i.e.\ the inverse association was somewhat stronger in its output. This difference is explained by the sample: at its default minimum of five comparison days \texttt{sleepreg} scored only participants with seven consecutive recorded days. The formula on the same 3673 participants gives the same fully adjusted factor as \texttt{sleepreg} ($0.938$ on the released series), and among the 60\% of participants with seven consecutive days the association was stronger (fully adjusted factor $0.946$ with the formula on the released series, \texttt{check\_subgroups.R}). The ordering of the input variants was the same for every calculator, from the released series to the main sleep period alone. The sample on which \texttt{sleepreg} could estimate the association was half of the published sample at its default setting and three quarters at three days. The participants lost were those with a gap in the recording (29.5\% of the sample) or with fewer than seven complete days (10.5\%).

\section{The classifier behind \texttt{PAXPREDM} and the derivation settings}
\label{sec:S_classifier}
\subsection{Identification of the classifier}
The NHANES documentation does not name the algorithm behind \texttt{PAXPREDM}. It describes an open-source machine-learning classification of the 80\,Hz signal into wake wear, sleep wear and non-wear in three steps [16]. In the first step, signal features are extracted from 1.5-minute windows and each 30-second window is classified, with a confidence value for each class. Stretches recorded in idle sleep mode (ISM) are replaced beforehand by the 10 seconds of signal that precede them, repeated over the stretch. In ISM the ActiGraph GT3X+ outputs a constant value during a period without movement until a threshold is exceeded on any axis [10]. The minute files record for every minute how many of its 4800 samples were taken in ISM (\texttt{PAXAISMM}). In the second step, periods shorter than a minimum duration (wake wear under 3 minutes, sleep wear and non-wear under 10 minutes) are reassigned on the basis of the confidence values of the adjacent periods. In the third step an orientation rule reassigns longer periods between non-wear and sleep. The 30-second resolution is explained by the polysomnography scoring of the validation data. This description corresponds step by step to the SWaN algorithm (sleep-wear, wake-wear and nonwear) of Thapa-Chhetry et al. [10], which was developed on ISM-filtered NHANES 2011--2014 recordings with polysomnography and directly observed wear as reference, validated against polysomnography and diaries, and which its authors state was used to generate the sleep summary measures of the survey. The data descriptor of a public derivative of the minute files attributes \texttt{PAXPREDM} to the same publication [15]. The public implementation (\texttt{swan\_accel} 1.18, Python) is maintained by the first author of that publication. Whether the released classification was produced with this version of the code and with the ISM replacement described above is not documented.

\subsection{Settings and minute labels}
\texttt{GGIR} 3.3-6 was run as listed in Table~\ref{tab:S_versions}. The 5-second epoch classes of part~5 were mapped to minutes as follows. A minute has twelve epochs. It is missing when more than half of them are flagged as non-wear, otherwise sleep when more than half of its twelve epochs are sleep, and wake otherwise. For \texttt{GGIR} SIB, sleep is the class \texttt{spt\_sleep} (inside the detected sleep period and inside a sustained inactivity bout). For \texttt{GGIR} SPT, sleep is every epoch inside the detected sleep period. SWaN was run with \texttt{swan\_first\_pass} on each hourly file (feature extraction and classification of 30-second windows) and \texttt{swan\_second\_pass} on each day (minimum-duration and orientation rules). A minute took the common label of its two 30-second windows when they agreed and was unknown otherwise. Non-wear and unknown minutes are missing. The ISM stretches were used as recorded, without the replacement by the preceding 10 seconds. Minute $k$ of a derived series (minutes counted from the first sample of the recording) was matched to the released minute with sample counter $\texttt{PAXSSNMP}/4800 = k$. All series were restricted to the days retained in the rebuilt series.

\section{Sample, computing platforms and stability of the derivation}
\label{sec:S_platforms}
\subsection{Random order and sample}
The 7085 participants of the published sample were placed in a random order with R's default generator and the seed \rawSeed{} and their raw-signal archives were processed in that order. The script \texttt{make\_random\_order.R} and the file \texttt{analytic\_random\_order.tsv} in the companion repository hold the order. The fixed order allows interim analyses on the participants with the smallest ranks whose series are complete. The analyses in the main text use the first \rawPrefixR{} participants of the order. For \rawNexcl{} of them (NHANES identifiers \rawExclSeqn) SWaN did not finish in 60 minutes in two attempts and these participants were excluded, which leaves \rawNsample{} participants with all four series.

\subsection{Computing platforms and equality between platforms}
Table~\ref{tab:S_platforms} lists the platforms. The cloud machines ran a container built from \texttt{rocker/r-ver:4.6.1} with the same versions of \texttt{GGIR} and of all 109 R packages as the laptops and a Python 3.8 environment with the pinned SWaN dependencies. The container definition is in the companion repository.

R's \texttt{cumsum()} accumulates in the C type \texttt{long double}, which is 8 bytes on Apple processors and 16 bytes (80-bit extended precision) on x86-64. The autocalibration of \texttt{GGIR} (\texttt{g.calibrate}) computes rolling means of the 10-second window features with \texttt{cumsum()} and then removes windows whose features equal those of the preceding window, a rule that targets repeated constant signal such as the ISM stretches. The number of windows removed differed between the two precisions, the calibration coefficients differed in consequence, the calibrated accelerations differed by up to approximately 3\% and the z-angle by up to 0.1 degrees. For one of six test participants the sleep/wake labels of some minutes differed (SRI 1.5 and 2.5 points higher for \texttt{GGIR} SIB and SPT on x86-64). SWaN was unaffected (identical classes, class probabilities that differed by at most 0.013). For the cloud runs the accumulation inside \texttt{g.calibrate} was therefore replaced by double-precision accumulation (a short patch loaded before \texttt{GGIR}, included in the repository), which reproduced the minute labels of the Apple laptop. Equality was verified on every participant processed both on the Apple M5 Max laptop and on another machine (15 on AWS, 26 on the two Hetzner server types, 23 on Vultr and 16 on the Apple M1 Pro laptop, which needs no patch): the sleep/wake minute labels of all three derived series were identical to the laptop's, the calibrated accelerations differed by at most 0.1\,mg and the z-angle by at most 0.003 degrees, and SWaN returned identical classes with smoothed class probabilities that differed by up to 0.46 in single participants (\texttt{platform\_check\_labels.R}, results in \texttt{validate/platform\_check/}).

\section{Model 4: interactions with sex and race/ethnicity}
\label{sec:S_model4}
Model 4 of the earlier study regresses log BMI on SRI with sex $\times$ SRI and race/ethnicity $\times$ SRI interactions and the covariates of Model 2, survey-weighted. For each race/ethnicity and sex group the multiplication factor for the top versus the bottom SRI quintile is $\exp(\beta_g\,\Delta)$, where $\beta_g$ is the group's SRI slope and $\Delta$ the median SRI difference between the top and bottom quintile of the input. The intervals are percentile intervals from the same 200-resample participant bootstrap as for Models 1 to 3. The earlier study reported the bootstrap means of 100 resamples as point estimates. The refit of the published SRI differs from its Table~3 by at most \mfourPubMaxDiffOrig{} in the twelve groups. Table~\ref{tab:S_model4} gives the factors for the published SRI and for the Phillips formula on the released and the four smoothed inputs. On the same input, \texttt{GGIR} agreed with the formula to \mfourMaxDiffGgir{} or less in every group. \texttt{sleepreg} with its default of five days differed by up to \mfourMaxDiffSleepregFive{} and with three days by up to \mfourMaxDiffSleepregThree{}, the largest differences in the smallest groups, because it returns a value for fewer participants (Table~\ref{tab:S_assoc}). The sex pattern held in every input: within each race/ethnicity group the factor of women was below that of men in \mfourWomenBelowMen{} comparisons. Smoothing moved the group factors by $\mfourShiftLo$ to $\mfourShiftHi$.

Figures~\ref{fig:S_model4_raw} and \ref{fig:S_model4_spt} repeat the earlier study's Figure~2 for the released series and for the main-sleep-period input: predicted BMI over the SRI range from 0 to 100 by race/ethnicity and sex, with the other covariates held at the published sample's means (continuous) and most frequent levels (categorical) and with 95\% bands from the bootstrap coefficient vectors. The SRI range extends beyond the observed values at both ends, as in the earlier study.

Table~\ref{tab:S_model4_raw} gives Model 4 on the random sample for the released classification and the three derived series. The groups hold \mfourRawNmin{} to \mfourRawNmax{} participants. The intervals are wide (widths \mfourRawWidthMin{} to \mfourRawWidthMax{}). Women had a lower factor than men in all six race/ethnicity groups for each of the four series.
